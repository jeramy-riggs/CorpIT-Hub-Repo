Enterprise Approval Portal

Requestor Quick Start Guide

A field-by-field walkthrough for every form type in the Enterprise Approval Portal. Follow each step in order, and use the examples as a template for your own submission.

🗺️ Which Form Do I Need?

Use the table below to pick the right form before you start.

Out-of-Country Access (OCA)

Traveling internationally and need access to company systems from abroad.

AI Use Case Intake (AIA)

Proposing a new AI solution, automation, integration, agent, or any AI use a business process will depend on. Goes through the approval chain.

Claude Access – Artificial Intelligence Policy Acknowledgment

You just need Claude for your own productivity. Read the AI Policy, tick the provisions, sign — no approval chain; IT provisions your access.

Website / URL Access (WUA)

A website or domain is blocked by IT and you need it for your work.

Privileged / Admin Access (PAA)

Need admin, root, or elevated access to a system or server.

Security Exception Request (SER)

Need to bypass a standard security control for a documented business reason.

IT Security Incident Form (SIR)

Reporting a security incident that has already occurred. Submit as soon as possible.

1

Business Justification

Step 2 of 4
Why is this exception required? *

Explain the business need driving this exception. Include the project, the timeline, the business impact of NOT receiving the exception, and why no compliant alternative exists.

Example
The vendor integration for Project ATLAS requires an unencrypted FTP connection to a legacy client system that does not support SFTP or HTTPS. This integration generates $2.4M in quarterly client revenue. The client has confirmed they will not upgrade their system until Q2 2027. We have no alternative mechanism to transfer the required data files. Exception requested for 6 months while a long-term secure solution is engineered in parallel.
Which standard control(s) cannot be followed? *

Reference the specific ADC security policy or control number that cannot be met. If you don't know the policy number, describe the control (e.g., "encryption-in-transit requirement").

Example
ADC Security Policy §4.2 – Data Encryption in Transit: requires TLS 1.2+ for all data transfers. The legacy client FTP system does not support TLS and cannot be upgraded within the project timeline.
What will be accessed or used? *

Describe exactly what systems, data, or resources will be involved in this exception.

Example
Nightly batch file transfers of property auction results (CSV format) to the client's legacy FTP server at 203.0.113.45. Files contain auction IDs, bid amounts, and property addresses — no PII or financial account data.
Who will use this exception? *
Example
2 members of the Data Integration team (ETL engineers John Smith and Maria Garcia)
How often will it be used? *
Example
Nightly automated job, 11:00 PM ET, Mon–Fri

1

Incident Details

Step 2 of 5
Date Incident Occurred *

The date the incident happened — not the date you discovered it. If you're unsure, use your best estimate and note the uncertainty in your description.

Example
2026-08-15
Time Incident Occurred *

Use 24-hour time in Eastern Time. If the incident was ongoing over a period, use the start time.

Example
14:32 (2:32 PM ET)
Criticality Rating *

Select the severity level that best matches the incident. When in doubt, rate it higher — InfoSec will downgrade if appropriate.

Scale
🔴 SEVERE — Data breach, ransomware, complete system compromise, regulatory-reportable event
🟠 HIGH — Significant data exposure, partial system compromise, active attacker
🟡 MODERATE — Contained unauthorized access, policy violation with limited impact
🟢 LOW — Near-miss, minor policy violation, no data exposed
Executive Summary *

A brief, non-technical summary of what happened. This is the first thing the CTO and CEO will read. Keep it to 2–4 sentences.

Example
On Aug 15 at 2:32 PM, an employee clicked a phishing link that compromised their M365 credentials. The attacker accessed the employee's email inbox for approximately 45 minutes before IT Security detected and terminated the session. No client data was accessed. The compromised account has been reset and additional MFA verification applied.
Root Cause *

What directly caused the incident? Be factual and objective — not accusatory.

Example
Employee received a convincing phishing email appearing to come from IT department requesting credential re-verification. The phishing page was not caught by existing email filters due to a newly registered domain.
Action Steps Taken During Incident *

List the containment actions taken while the incident was occurring, in chronological order.

Example
1. IT Security alerted at 3:15 PM via SIEM alert.
2. Compromised account disabled at 3:18 PM.
3. Active session terminated at 3:19 PM.
4. Employee notified and device isolated at 3:25 PM.
5. Password reset and MFA re-enrollment completed at 3:45 PM.
Remediation Plan *

What steps are being taken to prevent this type of incident from happening again?

Example
Phishing domain submitted to blocklist. All-hands security awareness email distributed. Targeted phishing simulation scheduled for affected department. Email filter rules updated to flag newly registered domains.
Services Impacted *

List every service affected and the name of its service manager.

Example
Service: Microsoft 365 Email (Outlook) — Service Manager: Rey Abad
Service: SharePoint (read access) — Service Manager: Alex Torres
2

Impact & Outcome

Step 3 of 5
What occurred and who was involved *

Full narrative of the incident — what happened step-by-step, who was involved (by role, not necessarily by name), and the timeline.

Example
At 2:32 PM on Aug 15, an Account Manager in the Sales department received a phishing email... [continue with full narrative]. The IT Security team identified the anomalous login from an unrecognized IP at 3:14 PM via SIEM alert and initiated containment immediately.
Who and what was affected *

Detail the full impact — people, systems, data, and any clients or customers involved. Be as complete as possible.

Example
1 employee account compromised. Email inbox (inbox only — sent items and drafts not accessed based on audit logs). No client records, loan data, or customer PII accessed. No client notifications required at this time. Impact confined to the compromised employee's Microsoft 365 account.
How was the incident resolved? *

Describe the resolution and current status. Is the incident fully contained? Are there any remaining risks?

Example
Incident fully contained as of 4:00 PM Aug 15. Compromised account credentials reset, MFA re-enrolled, and access reviewed. No ongoing risk identified. Email audit log review complete — no sensitive data forwarded or exfiltrated.
List Relevant Remediation *

All remediation steps, both completed and planned. Include target completion dates for planned items.

Example
✅ Compromised credentials reset (completed 8/15)
✅ Phishing domain blocked (completed 8/15)
⏳ Security awareness training re-assigned to Sales team (due 8/29)
⏳ Email filter update to block newly registered domains (due 8/22)
3

Security Analysis

Step 4 of 5
Security implications and controls that failed *

Technical details for the InfoSec team — what was accessed, what controls failed or were bypassed, and what the attacker could have done with the access they obtained.

Example
Attacker gained read access to M365 email inbox via stolen session token (OAuth). MFA was bypassed via AiTM phishing (attacker acted as proxy). Existing email filter failed to block the phishing domain (registered 6 hours prior to attack). Conditional Access policy for unrecognized IPs generated a SIEM alert but did not auto-block — gap identified in response playbook.
Detailed root cause analysis *

Thorough RCA including contributing factors, exact timeline, and detailed resolution steps. This is the technical record used for compliance and future prevention.

Example
Contributing factors: (1) Email filter relies on domain reputation scores — new domain had no history; (2) Conditional Access policy does not auto-block, only alerts; (3) Employee lacked awareness of AiTM phishing techniques.

Timeline: 14:30 — Phishing email delivered. 14:32 — Employee clicks link. 14:33 — Credentials harvested via proxy. 14:34 — Attacker establishes session. 15:14 — SIEM alert triggered (40-min dwell time). 15:18 — Account disabled...

Resolution: Session token revoked via Azure AD. Conditional Access policy updated to require re-authentication for new IP logins rather than just alerting.

Use "Track Your Request" on the Portal Home

From the portal homepage (accessportal.nrpi.local), click Track Your Request. Type your Request ID into the box — for example OCA-2026-00012 — then click Track Request →.

You will see the request ID, the form type, the status, and an Approval Steps timeline showing exactly which approver it is sitting with. Each step is marked Approved, Rejected, Awaiting review, Returned for changes, or Pending.

🔑

The tracker deliberately shows no personal information: it does not display the answers you gave or the approvers’ comments. If you need the reason behind a decision, contact the approver or the IT Service Desk.

📌

You can also see your request ID in the confirmation screen after submission — the format is AAA-YYYY-NNNNN, for example OCA-2026-00012. Save it — the request ID is the only way to track your request.

🔄 Understanding Request Statuses

Here's what each status means and what action — if any — you need to take.

Pending

Your request is with an approver. The tracker shows which step it is waiting at. No action needed from you — check back in 1–2 business days if you haven't heard anything.

Approved

All approvers have approved your request. IT will provision access shortly. If you don't have access within 24 hours, contact the IT Service Desk.

Rejected

An approver declined the request and it is now closed. You receive an email with the decision. You may submit a new request addressing the concerns, or escalate through your manager.

Returned

An approver sent it back because more information is needed. You receive an email naming the step that returned it and quoting exactly what the approver asked for. The request itself is closed, so read the email and submit a new request that addresses the point, quoting the original request ID.

📧
Every decision reaches you by email. Whichever way an approver decides — Approved, Rejected or Returned — the portal emails you automatically, with the request ID, the status, the step that decided, and the approver’s comment where one was given. You are not emailed as the request passes each intermediate step, so use Track Your Request if you want to see where it currently sits.

⚡ Tips for Faster Approval

  • 1Use the Directory Lookup on Step 1 — pre-filled forms with verified employee data are processed faster, and it is what captures your manager’s address so the request routes to the right person.
  • 2Write a specific justification. Vague answers like "needed for work" are the #1 cause of a request being “Returned”. Name the project, the client, or the deadline.
  • 3Notify your manager verbally before submitting for urgent requests. Approvers who know a request is coming process it faster.
  • 4For OCA and PAA forms, submit at least 7 business days before the need date. Last-minute requests receive the same review time — they just arrive too late.
  • 5List every system and data type upfront. Discovering you missed something after approval requires a new form.
  • 6For SER requests, include compensating controls — IP restrictions, audit logging, encryption at rest. These give approvers confidence that risk is being mitigated even without full compliance.
  • 7Save your request ID. It is the only way to track the request — there is no “my requests” list to fall back on.
  • 8Finish the form in one sitting. There is no draft save.
